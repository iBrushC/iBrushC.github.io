<i>Note, this article is in retrospect of my experience, going up until mid-2025</i>

<br><br>

When I first entered university in NCSU, I was eager to get into research because I'd previously done some independent dives and was interested in doing it in a professional setting. The university didn't have too much engineering research that interested me, so I figured I would reach out in the life sciences instead and apply to as many labs as possible. For one of them, the Sozzani Lab, I had a single interview and was invited to join. It was a bizarrely technical interview, which I later learned was because they had thought I was a grad student somehow.

<br><br>

Entering the lab, I was paired with the only two grad students in the lab to work on computational projects, developing neural networks and other programs to help with the lab. I did a good amount of research learning about Arabidopsis Thaliana, the subject of work for the lab, and some reading on RNNs, GNNs, and an at-the-time newer architecture called Transformers. 

<br><br>

I did minor help with running scripts, offering input and direction, and a few other coding things. Then at one point, I was looking back to back at transformers and a paper on master regulators and noticed that full attention and connectivity graphs for genes (gene regulator networks) looked awfully similar. I raised the point to my supervisor and just like that, I was given my own project to work on.

<br><br>

To cut a long story short, this was a long, painful learning experience for me. After the first semester, I had zero technical mentors and was approaching a tremendously difficult task entirely on my own as someone new to the field. I read through between 10-20 papers per week for months on end, spend hours playing with real and simulated datasets for training, and constructed and deconstructed every architecture under the sun. The conclusion is despite being called "Causal Attention", causal inference (self attention and cross attention) were absolute terrible predictors of connectivity. Having validated and revalidated my results, the 1.25 years of work resulted in showing the hypothesis was false. At this point, I was working remotely and already studying abroad in Japan, and decided to go on a temporary leave which soon became permanent as I prepared to transfer universities.

<br><br>

Though I didn't get conclusive results and my hypothesis wasn't validated, in the end it was still highly valuable. It showed me how to stick with long projects, tackle enormous amounts of research, formulate testing procedures, and much more. I have so much to owe to Ross Sozzani, who really helped teach me how to think like a researcher. I never truly said goodbye to her which is one of my only regrets about leaving NCSU.
