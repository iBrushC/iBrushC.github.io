All that to say, I like understanding things as other things. Recently, I've been in a lot of group scenarios and have been seeing more and more similarities between people and molecular dynamics. This article is just a quick collection of these similarities that I've noticed.

<br>
<h2>Annealing</h2>

 I've been going to way more mixers, parties, and community dinners than ever before and rediscovered that (obviously) you either build a few stronger connections, or many weak connections. The more interesting aspect is that, typically, a top-down view of the venue will show heads moving like metals during annealing.

<br><br>

In metal annealing, a piece of metal is heated to just below its melting point (recrystallization point for the nerds) which allows the atoms to move around and settle into more favorable arrangements. This typically reduces internal stress and increases flexibility.

<br><br>

The core process here is allowing atoms to move to more favorable locations. Adding energy to the metal (heating) allows them to move around somewhat freely: those in high stress wander, those in low stress settle in.

<br><br>

This is the same process I noticed happening at several mixers. At the beginning, energy is high, people move around, bump into each other, talk, and form bonds. But not all these bonds are favorable. Thankfully, the energy stays high for a decent amount of time, and the impurities work themselves out of their 3-8 person circles and into new ones. As the energy slowly decreases, those who aren't trying to optimize for Linkedin connections tend to find their subunit (generally 2-5 people for me). Allowing myself to settle into the conversations/people I just enjoyed the most both reduces internal stress and decreases bond fragility. 

<br><br>

I've personally found this to be true, and that if I spend a conference or hackathon talking to literally everyone, I don't walk out with a single connection I feel I could call on. Simply allowing myself to sit and enjoy the conversations with the people I mesh with has done wonders.

<h2>Graphite</h2>

Lets say you're walking to/from a concert along a somewhat narrow path. As long as it's not too crowded, people split into layers when walking.

<br><br>

 They're side-by-side with friends and family, but try to keep a healthy distance between their layer and those in front and behind. <b>This is the structure of graphite</b>, used in every pencil on earth. 

<br>

<div class="post-image" style="background-image: url('https://www.eastcarb.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Key-Elements-that-make-Structure-for-Graphite.png')"></div>

<br>

The layers of graphene are incredibly strong, yet form only loose, easily breakable links with other layers. Similarly, you'd be hard-pressed to separate a family leaving a small venue, but could very easily direct one family away from another.

<h2>Viscosity</h2>

Now imagine a more crowded scenario, much more croweded. People are flooding into a Taylor Swift concert or something. In this scenario, the graphite model no longer applies, and people behave much more like a fluid.

<br>

<div class="post-image" style="background-image: url('https://i.ytimg.com/vi/Mi7lQ7pFJ5k/maxresdefault.jpg')"></div>

<br>

People flow like the imaginary particles often used to represent the continuum of a fluid. Just like in the flow of a pipe, people pushing in a crowd are generally fastest in the center (note, this only applies for single directional flow) with those on the edges being pushed and caught on divets and extrusions.

<br>

<div class="post-image" style="background-image: url('https://nuclear-power.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/viscous-flow-velocity-profile.png')"></div>

<br>

Identically, flow through a pipe has the highest velocity at the center, as internal frictional forces (viscosity) drags the flow into a parabola. This happens because of the no slip condition on the edges resulting in (theoretically) zero velocity particles which then slow down the particles next to them and so on.

<br><br>

This isn't exactly one-to-one though, especially because of the method of slowing towards the edges. In liquid, frictional forces adhere to the edges, whereas in people, often the people pushing in the center create outward forces, causing a pressure buildup near the edges. In an ideal fluid and pipe model, there are no radially outward forces.
