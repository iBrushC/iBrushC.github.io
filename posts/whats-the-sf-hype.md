Anyone involved in the tech, business, or startup scene right now knows San Francisco as the "place to be"; it's the holy land to which all those worthy must make pilgrimages. I remember calling a good friend of mine from NYC who said she couldn't wait to get back there after graduating, which was bewildering because of how nice NYC is. But after spending an impromptu 3 weeks there with zero plans, I think I understand most (but not all) of the hype.

<br><br>

<h3>A Young Person's Paradise</h3>
Coming from a downstream-wildfire struck-hellscape of NYC with 100+ degree weather, the weather was obviously a huge plus. This level of pleasant temperatures and minimal humidity year-round is almost fantastical in the mind of an Ithaca-dwelling student, with maybe a single bad weather day in the time I was there. Although, I can't help but to feel this heaven is partly wasted on tech bros proudly flaunting their "single till series b" caps who would recoil at the idea of a day off in the sun.

<br><br>

Of course, the real reason you make the pilgrimage isn't the physical environment, it's the social environment. To those who involve themselves in it, the culture is aggressively fast paced, being as stable or dime-turning as you make it. You can see some guy go from being unemployed to founding engineer to fired just by regularing a cafe for a few weeks. People will say absurd phrases like "I quit being CTO of this $XXm company because I was bored" (real quote from a guy I'd just met) which you simply wouldn't hear anywhere else. More than any governing body, SF is principally run by the exponential distribution and moonshots, disproportionately rewarding (and sometimes destroying) those with high agency and ambition.

<br><br>

In both business and social circles, my overall thoughts on what makes SF so appealing to young people (my thesis of sorts) is that

<blockquote>
    <i>The barrier for entry for almost anything is a single conversation.</i>
</blockquote>

In other words, you can get involved in practically anything you set your mind to extremely easily (at least in comparison to many other places). Especially with the startup boom happening right now, money is being thrown around and people are hiring and firing for any or no reason. If you're skilled in something and can talk to people, you can get hired in SF.

<br><br>

Even ignoring the business side of things, the social barrier for entry is sometimes terrifyingly low. The aforementioned money being thrown around means that a random Luma event will get you a free fancy dinner at some rooftop, which can easily snowball into 4-5 other invitations (peptide parties are all the rage it seems, as I got invited to four while I was there). As an illustration of this, I went to a community dinner, which got me invited to an accelerator, which got me invited to a dinner, which got me invited to a demo day on a battleship. On a different day, I won a bottle of wine while playing Mafia with a group of 20-30 year olds.

<br><br>

There's also other scattered quirks that I like about it, including the more open layout (as compared to NYC), the serviceable public transportation, the wildlife, and more, but I don't feel strongly enough about any of them to go into more details.

<h3>Superficiality</h3>
For those not as laser-focused on networking, SF almost certainly feels terrible. Because of that "one conversation away" thing I mentioned in the thesis, chats of any substance are practically always followed by "let me get your Linkedin" in the hopes that you just had breakfast with a big VC who's going to angel your next big AI B2B SaaS. All events are networking events, whether they say it or not. People will be scoping you out from the second you walk over to them till the next day when they psychoanalyze your work experience.

<br><br>

For those who are willing to take advantage of it, this is pretty great. You talk to hundreds of NPCs a day and eventually will land on a few that progress the storyline a bit further. There's no fear of embarrassing yourself or ruining reputations because there are simply too many people. You spill your drink on a multi-billionaire? No worries, just hop on over to the next yacht party and try again. It's extremely freeing, but also comparatively lonely if you come from a city like NYC or Boston which has a bit more soul to it.

<br><br>

One thing I would like to comment on, despite not fully participating in it, is the dating scene for men. I had heard a lot about how impossible it was and how it's so bad that people move elsewhere, find a girlfriend, then bring them back. I personally think this is a skill issue which can be chalked up to tech bros not knowing how to talk to women. The ratios are already 8-1 or worse, and men just seem to inexplicably avoid women. At many events I was one of maybe three men that approached women to have normal conversations. Incredible things happen when you don't immediately shill your startup to whoever will listen.

<h3>Finding New Ideas</h3>
A week into being there, I made the finalized decision that I was going to quit the startup I had been working on for the past 5 months. Hundreds of hours and several thousand dollars amounting to nothing, but it was easy after the discussions I was having. The superficiality makes it quite easy to have friendly fast-paced brainstorming sessions with strangers, and after about a dozen different topics -- all of which excited me more than my current idea -- I decided there's too much exciting stuff out there to be working on something I didn't enjoy. I'm taking a short break before full-diving into another project, but I have good leads and a handful of things that excite me far more than my previous project.

<h3>Closing Thoughts</h3>
On one of my last days there, I met with a senior engineer at some large company who asked me to message him when I'm back in the city. Multiple people throughout the trip made up their mind that this was the place that I belonged and I would find my way back eventually. Whether or not that will turn out to be true (still gunning for Singapore) will be decided in the next 1-3 years, but I see where they're coming from, and I somewhat agree. I can envision myself there, working there, and living the constant shenanigans I got a trial run of. The endless creativity, opportunities, and conversations with strangers seem up my alley.
