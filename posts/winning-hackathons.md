Title: Doing Well In Hackathons
Description: My experience with a doing hackathons in San Francisco
Image: hackathon.jpg
Tags: Project
Date: July 2026
Content:

In my three weeks in San Francisco I went to 5 hackathons, won one, and was an honorable mention in another (an organizer said we would have been a finalist if not for doing a hardware project in a software hackathon).

<br><br>

We (a friend and I) won a hackathon by building a travel app, using the sponsors' platforms to make a fast information-retrieval system that fed into a conversational chat bot. It worked so well that it actually surprised us, even if we only got it working 15 minutes before the demo. An idea we thought of day-of is now one of my favorite project sprints, and we're working on turning it into an actual app now. Time pressure does wonders for creativity.

<br><br>

I signed up for the hackathons mostly as a time-filler since I had a few days with nothing planned and figured they'd be fun. I'd never done a software/engineering related hackathon before so I was going into all of them blind, but they were much les stressful and competitive than I thought. 

<br><br>

Of course, I was signing up for small-scale local competitions, but I had previous imagined that hackathons were fiece competition. On the contrary, it seems these are mostly collective trauma events of everyone sumbitting patchwork projects with the most creative presentation winning. It's a very fun experience, but it has made me significantly less impressed when I see "12x hackathon winner" on someone's Linkedin.

<br><br>

Outside of that, my general learnings can be written pretty simply as:

<ol>

<li>Get something working first. First two were made infinitely worse by creating a bunch of scattered systems and trying to merge them all at the end, which is a lot of avoidable stress.</li>
<li>Idea is the most important. Technical details and implementation can be ignored in a 5 minute demo, having a fun idea cannot. The people that win often have useful products with fun demos.</li>
<li>Use Chinese coding models. Claude and Codex write good code, but you only need fast code. I've had a much better experience getting things implemeted in a timely manner using Minimax M3, which I would recommend for people working within 5 hour time windows.</li>

</ol>

Even after leaving I think I'll participate in more hackathons, mostly for the social aspect. Now with vibe coding, there's a ton of downtime for mingling and whatnot, which makes them just as much networking events as competitions. Winning is a nice bonus too, I'll try to do that more in the future as well.
